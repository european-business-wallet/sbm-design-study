# Review agenda — the open questions, stated so they can be answered

This is the list a reviewer should read before the specification, because it
says where the specification does **not** yet have an answer. Each entry names
the question, what the specification currently assumes, which claim depends on
the answer, and whose expertise would settle it. Nothing here is hidden
elsewhere: every legal question below is also marked, visibly, where it applies
in the normative text as `TODO(legal)`.

A question being on this list is not a defect in the review edition. A question
being open and **not** on this list would be.

## Protocol and architecture

| # | Question | Current assumption | Claim that depends on it | Expertise |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | **How does the RDP that issues a DE obtain the Delivery Service's receipt — and which RDP issues the DE when sender and recipient use different providers?** | None published. The DS contract says the issuer MUST verify the receipt; the only way the receipt leaves the DS is back to the acknowledging device. The contract names RDP(out) as issuer; TS clause 4.1 names the recipient-side RDP. Having the wallet forward the receipt is **not** safe: a recipient could suppress delivery evidence by never forwarding it, which defeats the availability grade. | Availability-grade DE; every DE in the four-corner deployment | Protocol and security design; QERDS operators |
| A2 | **What maximum age should a live decision accept for a membership assertion?** | The MODEL is settled: live use is a lease, decided once at the start of the exchange on the deciding provider's clock, against a record that states `admitted`, was not asserted after that instant and is no older than the maximum the Federation Authority publishes. Only the number is open. Historical verification needs no such bound — an assertion speaks up to its own `asserted_at`, and `as_of` evaluates a complete record rather than truncating one. | Live admission checks between providers | Federation governance; operations |
| A3 | **How can a verifier know a retained acceptance-policy chain is complete** — that no later version existed? | It cannot, from inside a bundle: every shipped bundle reports this as INCOMPLETE. Closing it needs an authenticated log head — the same missing primitive as key transparency. | "The governing policy was the latest in force"; detection of provider withholding | Transparency systems; applied cryptography |
| A4 | **How should the post-quantum hybrid suite be tested across implementations?** | Pinned to draft-ietf-mls-pq-ciphersuites-06 under a private-use code point (0xF5C1). The draft publishes no test vectors and this repository implements no ML-KEM, so no cross-implementation KEM vector exists. | Post-quantum confidentiality of any group that selects the suite | MLS and post-quantum implementers |
| A5 | **Who publishes and updates a group's retained state?** | `GET /groups/{group_id}/context` is published and nothing publishes the bytes it serves; declared as a residual. | Retained verification of the cipher-suite decision and `mls_state` | MLS Delivery Service implementers |
| A6 | **The MSP as a federation participant** — its identifier, its contract, and the binding of a DE to the MSP's observation. | Decided in principle and **planned, not implemented**: the register admits only `rdp`, and no Schema carries `MspId` or `observed_by`. What is planned is the MSP's own identity on the wire, its admission to the register, and the DE's binding to the observation it rests on (`observed_by`, `receipt_digest`). | Separated MSP/RDP deployments | Implementers; operators |
| A7 | **A cross-deployment contract for the wallet-agent interface.** | The agent's evidence and mandate rules are specified (profile 5, Annex R); the interface between deployments is informative only. | Profile-5 interoperability | Wallet and agent implementers |
| A8 | **Mapping to heterogeneous registered-delivery systems.** | Homogeneous federations only; the relay-stage mapping onto ETSI EN 319 522 is the study's own reading. | Any gateway to another QERDS | ETSI ESI; QERDS providers |
| A9 | **Who observes S2, and what can an MSP acting alone make an RDP attest?** | S2 is the Delivery Service's signed receipt over a device acknowledgement made in a device-authenticated session with a collection token the DS issued — not wallet-signed. So the MSP is a **trusted observer** of the handover: a malicious MSP could fabricate one, and the planned `observed_by` binding (A6) would make that attributable, not impossible. Three models are analysed, none chosen — an accountable MSP observer, a handover proof authenticated independently of the MSP, and the handover inside the RDP's boundary — in the **MSP and RDP separated** record's *Alternatives considered*, which travels with this study. **Nothing claims resistance to a malicious MSP** until one is chosen. | The availability grade in a separated deployment; any claim about MSP compromise | Security design; QERDS operators |
| A10 | **Which Delivery Service routes a group, and who owns its membership after formation?** | A DS routes the devices it observed joining: the founder registered with it and the devices invited through it. In the four-corner flow the creator deposits every invitation at the counterparty's DS, which therefore routes the whole group, the creator included; the creator's own DS routes only devices invited through it, so the acceptance the SE rests on may create no items there. Whether each entity's devices should be served by its own DS is not decided. After formation, an Add arrives as a new invitation and is routed like any other; a Remove — or a member leaving — changes no DS's delivery set, because nothing publishes it to the DS, so a removed device keeps receiving ciphertext it can no longer decrypt. | Device-scoped delivery after formation; separated DS stores | MLS Delivery Service implementers; protocol design |
| A11 | **How does a receiver say “I cannot compute this” — and where is a hash mode advertised?** | **Decided, 25 September 2026: every defined mode is mandatory to implement** — `raw-sha256`, `raw-sha512`, `manifest-sha256`, `manifest-sha512`. The Internet-Draft (*Canonicalisation and Payload Hashing*) states it, the TS carries ICS row 192, and `LINT-HASH-01` refuses a mode outside the profile. Before the decision: nothing published. The canonicalisation modes were removed on 25 September 2026 because a receiver that met one without an implementation could only assert a `payload-hash-mismatch` that had not occurred, or fall silent into NDE `expired`; removing them did not close the class, because `manifest-*` needs a construction `raw-*` does not and reproduced the same dead end. The decision removes the receiver that cannot comply rather than giving it a way to say so, which holds **only while no mode is added**: adding one reopens this entry, and the honest alternatives are then to advertise supported modes in BW-MED / BW-ORG and add an NDE reason for a mode that cannot be computed | Every multipart message; any future mode or extension a peer may meet — which is what would reopen this | Protocol design; registry governance |
| A12 | **Should the content digest be salted** — the salt carried in the encrypted envelope and revealed only with the content, as the grade commitment already is? | That a digest over plaintext discloses nothing useful. That holds where the plaintext has entropy and fails where it is guessable — an invoice on a known template, an amount in a narrow range. **The profile now says so rather than assuming it**: the I-D's Security Considerations carry *Guessable content behind an unsalted digest* and the umbrella's consolidated threat model (§11.1) lists it among what the metadata can reveal, both naming who can confirm a guess and who cannot, and both saying this question is open. So what is open is no longer whether to state the exposure but **whether stating it is enough**. It salts commitments (`grade_commitment_salt`, `mandate_commitment_salt`, the dispute `salt`, sixteen bytes each, fresh per message) and does not salt `payload_hash` or the envelope `content_digest`. | Every statement that evidence carries no content: it carries a digest of it, which is a test oracle for a guess — and under Mode C each part digest likewise. A change would reach the envelope schema, the evidence schemas, the recipient's re-verification rule, the multipart manifest and its per-part digests, and the reveal path at dispute. A construction that would answer it — the salt of its own, the content-named modes, the retention duty, the manifest's cleartext decided with it — is set out as a **proposal, not a decision**, in [SBM-ADR-0014](adr/SBM-ADR-0014.md). | Applied cryptography; QERDS operators, because a salted digest changes what a retained bundle proves without the salt |
| A13 | **Should a chunked part's digest ever be a Merkle root, and under which construction?** | **Decided, 27 September 2026: the question does not arise, because this profile has no chunked part.** Content cannot be transformed under end-to-end encryption, and **nothing in the profile splits a manifest part** — which is what this question turns on, and is true independently of any envelope framing. An earlier answer here cited the CE envelope transformations in support; they are not defined operations and are deferred as **A15**, so this closure rests on the part rule alone. A part's `digest` is therefore over that part's whole decoded octets whatever framing carried them, and **None is profiled**. Two sentences were withdrawn to get here: the Merkle root itself, on 27 September, which defined no chunk boundaries, leaf encoding, node hashing, domain separation, odd-node handling or discriminator, so two implementations given identical bytes could derive different roots while breaking no published rule; and, the same day, the permission that made it readable — "a very large part MAY be chunked in transport" — which named no descriptor, no evidence of a split and no size at which a part becomes large, and read *part* as content where the operation acts on the envelope. **What would have to come first if this reopens**, recorded so that it cannot return as a construction alone: a part-chunk descriptor, evidence of the split, a reassembly rule with a defined endpoint, and only then the construction — chunk boundaries, leaf and node encodings with domain separation, the odd-node rule, a discriminator in the descriptor, and vectors for an empty part, one part, several chunks, a short final chunk and an odd number of chunks. | Any very large attachment, if a later revision ever splits one below the part boundary; nothing today | Applied cryptography; implementers who carry large attachments — whose question, if they have one, is whether part-level chunking is wanted at all, not which construction |
| A14 | **How is a multipart payload laid out inside the envelope?** | **Decided, 27 September 2026.** The payload — the octets after the `0x00` separator in the MLS application data — is the deterministic-CBOR encoding of `payload-multipart`, an array of `{part_id, octets}` maps in byte-wise ascending `part_id` order whose `part_id` set equals the manifest's exactly. Keyed rather than positional, for the reason the manifest is a list of maps: a fixed-position encoding is one silent reordering away from attributing a part's octets to another part's descriptor. The payload is therefore **self-describing** — a recipient splits it without holding any evidence, and content parsing does not depend on the provider-sealed SE that carries the manifest. Mode A is unaffected: its payload is the octets. **Before the decision, nothing was published**: no delimiter, no length-prefix framing, no reference to an existing multipart format, and no statement that the parts were concatenated in canonical order at the declared lengths — which is what the reference generator assumed, from fixtures, without the profile saying so. Two independent implementations could satisfy every rule and fail to exchange one multipart message. | Every multipart (Mode C) message between independent implementations. **Why it survived a dozen rounds**: `payload_hash` is the digest of the MANIFEST, not of the plaintext, so every check a provider or a bundle verifier can run passes on one implementation's own bytes — and a recipient that recomputed only `payload_hash`, which is all the earlier re-verification rule asked, compared the manifest with itself. The rule now states both steps in order: each part's digest recomputed from the octets received, then the manifest digest against `payload_hash`. | Protocol design; implementers who carry attachments. Answered in the profile rather than referred out: the framing is a choice, and the profile's own canonicalisation already fixed which encoding to choose |
| A15 | **What is a Change-Indication Evidence transformation, exactly — and what commits to its outputs?** | **Deferred, 27 September 2026; an RDP MUST NOT issue either transformation until this is answered.** The CE enumeration carries `re-packaging` and `chunking` and commits to the input octets and to each output. Neither operation is defined, and the output commitment cannot express what chunking produces: `EnvelopeHash` is fixed to `mls10-message`, SHA-256 over a **complete** TLS-serialized MLSMessage, and a fragment of an MLSMessage is not one — while re-packaging an unchanged message in an outer wrapper leaves the inner octets untouched, so its output commitment equals its input. No published contract defines a fragment descriptor, a chunk order, a reassembly operation, or the boundary at which the original message is reconstructed and checked, so two providers given the same message could not perform the same transformation and no verifier could reproduce either. The shipped sample did not show otherwise: its CE values were digests of the strings `demo-part:<message_id>:1` and `demo-repack:<message_id>`, typed as MLSMessage hashes while being no MLSMessage at all — hashing real bytes does not put them in the declared domain. | Any deployment whose provider re-frames an envelope, and every verifier reading a CE. **A13 rests on this being deferred rather than defined**: that closure holds because nothing splits a manifest PART, which is true independently, and an earlier revision wrongly cited envelope framing as a defined operation in its support. | Protocol design; QERDS operators who re-frame. **What a definition must pin**: a transport-frame commitment of its own rather than `mls10-message`, the exact hashed octets, fragment metadata and order, the reassembly boundary, the relation to the invariant SE/relay/DE commitment to the complete message, how repeated transformations chain — the present rule binds every CE input to the original SE and not to a previous CE output — and a published trace: real input, real outputs, a reassembled message, every digest verified |

## Deferred deliverable — the implementer guide

Not a question, and not delivered. The maintainer deferred implementer-grade
flows to the architecture documentation that follows this review; until that
exists, an implementer has the contracts and the reference, and these four
things in neither of them:

| # | Missing | Where it will be answered |
|---|---|---|
| G1 | **The pre-join proof, exactly**: which KeyPackage key signs a Welcome refusal, the exact signed request bytes, the transport, and freshness and replay protection. **Answered, 27 September 2026** (the I-D, *Group Establishment*): the KeyPackage's **leaf signature key**, whose private half only the invited device holds and whose public half the Delivery Service verifies against **from the package it already holds** — a device that has joined nothing has no group, no discovery document and no wallet key; `SignWithLabel` of RFC 9420 §5.1.2 under the label `SBMWelcomeRefusal`, over the deterministic-CBOR typed array `["sm-mls:welcome-refusal:v1", welcome_id, keypackage_ref, offered_suite, required_floor, reason, nonce]`, so every value the refusal asserts is inside the signature; the transport unchanged; and a **single-use nonce** the Delivery Service issues with each Welcome, spent only when a refusal is ACCEPTED — a rejected one leaves the device able to try again — with an exact retry converging before the proof is examined. **What it replaced**: possession of `keypackage_ref`, a hash of the package's PUBLIC bytes held by the inviting creator and the Delivery Service alike — the two parties with a motive to forge a refusal attributed to the device. | Implementer guide; then the DS contract |
| G2 | **One complete trace through the public operations**, negative and retry branches included — reservation to evidence retrieval, both entities, several members. **Partly answered, 27 September 2026**: [`docs/implementer-trace.md`](implementer-trace.md) traces 31 steps through the published operations — formation, sending, delivery, confirmation and the multipart outcomes — with eleven negative and three retry branches, generated by driving the reference and regenerated by `make trace`. **What it still does not reach**: a live Delivery Evidence object (DE issuance runs off the receipt-and-state path, not off a published call), the four-corner relay hop, and any second implementation — the trace is the reference composing with itself, which is not an interoperability result. | Implementer guide |
| G3 | **Bootstrap and change**: key-role transitions, and a provider's migration or exit — draining queues, and who holds the retained evidence afterwards. **Partly answered, 27 September 2026.** The *transitions* half is now checked rather than asserted: every row of the lifecycle table's `Messages in flight` and `Evidence already issued` columns is a probe (`tests/test_lifecycle_claims.py`), and every claim holds — a replacement device inherits nothing and the old queue is untouched, a policy successor published after an act does not disturb the version the message pinned while one in force before it is reported, and a member suspended after the act keeps its acknowledgement. They hold for one structural reason worth naming: verification reads the documents a verifier was handed, never live state and never an endpoint — rewrite every endpoint in a discovery document to a different provider and a bundle verifies unchanged. The *custody* half is therefore the whole of the remaining question, and [`retrievability.md`](retrievability.md) states it per input: of seventeen verification inputs, **ten depend on material a provider exit leaves with no named server**, and exactly one historical read — the federation register's `?as_of=` — is served by a party that is not the exiting provider, which is the shape the others lack. Absence degrades a verdict to INCOMPLETE and never to a silent pass, so this is a specification gap, not a defect. **What it still does not do**: name that successor (no record carries a custody pointer — the only `custody` in the tree is hardware key custody), or give every in-flight item a documented fate at exit. Both are wire-visible and deliberately left as decisions. | Implementer guide; custody duties in the umbrella |
| G4 | **Who sees what**: metadata, privacy and retention visibility per actor (wallet, MSP, RDP, Federation Authority, directory). | Implementer guide |

Status: **deferred, not closed**. The maintainer owns it; the

## Questions for the call for feedback

- Is the split between entity, member, device, MSP, RDP and Federation
  Authority understandable and operationally plausible?
- Are the delivery grades, and the scope of what each evidence object claims —
  including the narrowed provider-attested modes — clear?
- Can an implementer derive every required request field and verification
  input from the published contracts and retained artefacts? Where not, that
  is a finding: the reviews have already found several (two
  fixed; one still open).
- Does the admission model — an assertion speaks up to its own `asserted_at` —
  handle suspension, recovery, key rotation and later verification?
- Are reservation consumption, group membership and retry rules sufficient
  for two independent implementations to build the same state machines?
- What minimum production wallet and credential profile, and what migration
  and retention duties, are needed?
- Which qualification, event-mapping and legal-effect claims need correction
  or external confirmation?
